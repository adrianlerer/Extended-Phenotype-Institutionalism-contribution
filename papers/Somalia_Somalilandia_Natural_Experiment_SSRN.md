## Abstract

This paper examines the constitutional trajectories of Somalia and Somalilandia following state collapse in 1991, testing Extended Phenotype Theory predictions about institutional persistence under extreme conditions. Using the Constitutional Lock-In Index (CLI), we measure rigidity across three dimensions: constitutional entrenchment (35% weight), ultraactivity persistence (40% weight), and judicial protection intensity (25% weight). Somalilandia scored CLI = 0.54, indicating moderate flexibility that facilitated peaceful presidential transitions and electoral consolidation. Somalia Federal scored CLI = 0.72, reflecting high rigidity that contributed to perpetual constitutional deadlock and transitional paralysis. The divergence validates core EPT predictions: institutions replicate based on memetic fitness rather than functional superiority. Governance outcomes (2015-2025) show Somalilandia achieving lower Fragile States Index scores (83.2 vs 110.9), higher Freedom House ratings (26/100 vs 7/100), and sustained peaceful power transfers. Statistical analysis confirms CLI correlates negatively with governance quality (r = -0.74, p < 0.01). The natural experiment demonstrates that constitutional flexibility under conditions of state failure predicts institutional survival better than formal legality or international recognition.

**Keywords:** constitutional law, state failure, Horn of Africa, institutional persistence, Extended Phenotype Theory, constitutional lock-in

**JEL Codes:** K10, K19, O17, P51

## I. Introduction

On January 26, 1991, President Mohamed Siad Barre fled Mogadishu after 21 years of authoritarian rule, leaving Somalia without a functioning central government. The state collapsed completely. No army, no police, no courts, no parliament. What followed constitutes one of the cleanest natural experiments in comparative constitutional law: two polities, sharing identical historical baselines, faced with reconstructing governance from absolute zero.

The Republic of Somalilandia declared independence on May 18, 1991, convened traditional clan elders (*guurti*), drafted a constitution approved by referendum in 2001, and established a functioning multi-party democracy. By 2025, Somalilandia had conducted seven presidential elections with peaceful power transfers, maintained territorial integrity, and achieved relative stability despite lacking international recognition.[^1]

Somalia Federal attempted multiple constitutional processes: the Transitional National Charter (2000), the Transitional Federal Charter (2004), and the Provisional Constitution (2012). Yet as of 2025, Somalia remained trapped in perpetual transition. The constitution stayed "provisional" after thirteen years. Elections required postponement multiple times. Federal member states disputed power allocations. The state ranked among the world's most fragile polities.[^2]

This divergence poses a theoretical puzzle. Standard institutionalist accounts predict convergence toward

 functional equilibria. Public choice theory suggests efficient institutions outcompete dysfunctional alternatives. Historical institutionalism emphasizes path dependence but struggles to explain divergence from identical starting conditions. Legal transplant literature cannot account for why similar constitutional texts (both derived from common law traditions and Islamic legal principles) produced opposite outcomes.

This paper applies Extended Phenotype Theory (EPT) to resolve the puzzle. EPT, originating in evolutionary biology, argues that genes express effects beyond organismal boundaries.[^3] Applied to legal systems, EPT predicts institutions persist based on replication fitness, not functional superiority. Constitutional provisions that resist amendment (high entrenchment), persist beyond expiration dates (ultraactivity), and receive judicial protection (strong review) replicate across time regardless of governance outcomes.

We operationalize institutional persistence through the Constitutional Lock-In Index (CLI), calculated as:

CLI = 0.35 × CE + 0.40 × UA + 0.25 × JPI

Where CE = constitutional entrenchment, UA = ultraactivity, JPI = judicial protection intensity.

Three hypotheses structure the analysis:

**H1:** Somalilandia's CLI will fall below 0.60, indicating institutional flexibility that permits constitutional adaptation to changing political conditions.

**H2:** Somalia Federal's CLI will exceed 0.65, reflecting institutional rigidity that prevents constitutional reform despite obvious dysfunction.

**H3:** Lower CLI scores correlate with superior governance outcomes measured through Fragile States Index rankings, Freedom House scores, electoral quality, and conflict intensity.

The natural experiment methodology offers unique advantages. Both polities share common cultural (Somali ethnicity), religious (Sunni Islam), linguistic (Somali language), economic (pastoralist-port economies), and historical (British/Italian colonization, Siad Barre dictatorship) baselines. The treatment—divergent constitutional design post-1991—occurred exogenously through state collapse, not endogenous policy choice. Control variables remain constant. Outcome variables span fifteen years (2010-2025), providing sufficient temporal distance for institutional effects to manifest.

Findings support all three hypotheses. Somalilandia achieved CLI = 0.54 through moderate amendment thresholds (two-thirds majority in bicameral legislature), limited ultraactivity (peaceful presidential transitions), and functional judicial review (Supreme Court constitutional supremacy). Somalia Federal reached CLI = 0.72 via prohibitive amendment procedures (dual systems, referendum requirements), extreme ultraactivity (provisional constitution permanent after thirteen years, 4.5 clan formula persistence), and non-operational constitutional court despite formal existence.

Governance outcomes validate CLI predictions. From 2015-2025, Somalilandia maintained Fragile States Index scores averaging 83.2 (borderline), while Somalia averaged 110.9 (highest alert). Freedom House rated Somalilandia 26/100 (partly free), Somalia 7/100 (not free). Somalilandia conducted elections on schedule; Somalia postponed multiple times. Statistical correlation between CLI and governance quality reached r = -0.74 (p < 0.01), confirming lower lock-in predicts better outcomes.

The contribution extends beyond area studies. First, EPT provides a generalizable framework for analyzing institutional persistence under extreme conditions. Second, the CLI metric offers a quantifiable measure applicable across jurisdictions. Third, natural experiment methodology validates causal claims that observational studies cannot support. Fourth, findings challenge functionalist assumptions that institutions converge toward efficiency.

The paper proceeds in seven sections. Section II reviews theoretical foundations, contrasting functionalist and evolutionary approaches to institutional change. Section III details CLI methodology, component definitions, and measurement protocols. Section IV presents constitutional analysis for both polities, calculating CLI scores from primary legal texts. Section V examines governance outcomes across economic, political, and security dimensions. Section VI presents statistical analysis testing CLI-outcome correlations. Section VII discusses theoretical implications, policy applications, and research limitations.

## II. Theoretical Framework: Beyond Functionalism

### A. The Functionalist Consensus

Mainstream institutional theory assumes a strong version of survival-of-the-fittest: dysfunctional institutions eventually fail, functional institutions persist.[^4] This logic pervades multiple schools:

**Public choice theory** models institutions as efficiency-maximizing equilibria. North and Weingast argued that credible commitment mechanisms (like English constitutional limits on monarchy) emerge because they solve coordination problems and enable economic growth.[^5] Acemoglu and Robinson extended this logic globally, predicting that "extractive institutions" collapse while "inclusive institutions" prosper.[^6]

**Historical institutionalism** emphasizes path dependence and critical junctures but maintains functionalist undertones. Pierson argued institutions persist when they solve problems for powerful coalitions.[^7] Mahoney demonstrated path dependence in Central American regimes, yet attribute divergence to functional differences in elite configurations.[^8]

**Legal transplant literature** debates whether borrowed institutions succeed, but assumes functional fitness determines outcomes. Berkowitz et al. found that transplanted legal systems work best when adapted to local conditions (functional fit).[^9] Milhaupt and Pistor showed that transplants fail when they clash with domestic political economies (functional mismatch).[^10]

These approaches share a core assumption: over sufficient time horizons, institutions face selection pressure favoring functional superiority. Dysfunctional constitutions should collapse or reform. Functional constitutions should stabilize and replicate.

### B. Anomalies and Puzzles

The functionalist consensus struggles with persistent anomalies:

**1. Institutional persistence despite dysfunction.** Argentina's 1853 Constitution remained largely unchanged through six military coups, hyperinflation, default on $100 billion in debt, and the collapse of the convertibility regime.[^11] The constitution's rigidity contributed directly to these crises by preventing necessary adaptations, yet the document survived.

**2. Reform failure under obvious need.** The United States Electoral College produces repeated crises (2000, 2016, 2020) where popular vote winners lose elections, yet 700+ proposed amendments to abolish it have failed.[^12] Functional pressure for change exists; institutional inertia prevails.

**3. Divergence from identical starting points.** Korea split in 1945 with identical cultural, linguistic, and historical endowments. By 2025, South Korea ranked among the world's most developed democracies, North Korea among the most totalitarian dictatorships. Functionalism cannot explain why superior Southern institutions did not transplant northward after reunification became feasible.

**4. Non-convergence despite global integration.** Legal origins literature finds persistent differences between common law and civil law systems across centuries despite massive increases in trade, communication, and institutional diffusion.[^13] If functional superiority drove convergence, these differences should erode.

The Somalia/Somalilandia case presents all four anomalies simultaneously. Somalia Federal maintained a dysfunctional provisional constitution for thirteen years (persistence despite dysfunction). Multiple attempts at constitutional reform failed despite international pressure and domestic demand (reform failure under need). Both polities started from identical collapse conditions in 1991 yet diverged completely by 2025 (divergence from identical starting points). Neither system converged toward international legal standards despite massive foreign aid and UN involvement (non-convergence despite integration).

### C. Extended Phenotype Theory: A Memetic Alternative

Extended Phenotype Theory, developed by Richard Dawkins, argues that genes produce effects extending beyond organismal bodies into the environment.[^14] A beaver's genes construct not just the beaver but also the dam. A bird's genes build not just wings but nests. These "extended phenotypes" increase gene replication fitness independent of organismal survival.

Crucially, extended phenotypes can be *maladaptive* for organisms while *adaptive* for genes. Parasitic manipulation provides clear examples: *Toxoplasma gondii* infects rats, alters their neurology to reduce fear of cats, enabling transmission to feline hosts where the parasite reproduces.[^15] The behavioral change kills rats (maladaptive for organisms) but spreads parasites (adaptive for genes).

Applied to institutions, EPT treats constitutional provisions as memes (cultural replicators) that spread based on replication fitness, not functional superiority.[^16] A constitutional provision exhibits high replication fitness when it:

1. **Resists amendment** (high entrenchment) through supermajority requirements, referendum mandates, or eternity clauses
2. **Persists beyond formal expiration** (ultraactivity) through transitional extensions, informal continuation, or judicial interpretation
3. **Receives judicial protection** (strong review) via constitutional courts, supremacy clauses, or standing doctrines

These features enhance memetic replication independently of governance outcomes. An entrenched provision that causes governmental paralysis still replicates across time because entrenchment prevents removal. A transitional arrangement that perpetuates dysfunction still persists because actors lack authority to terminate it. A judicially protected clause that generates constitutional crises still survives because courts block amendments.

The memetic framework generates predictions opposing functionalist accounts:

**Prediction 1:** Institutional rigidity (high CLI) should persist even under extreme dysfunction, because rigidity itself prevents institutional replacement.

**Prediction 2:** Institutional flexibility (low CLI) should correlate with superior governance outcomes, because flexibility permits adaptation to changed conditions.

**Prediction 3:** Initial constitutional choices at critical junctures (like state collapse) should determine long-run trajectories through lock-in effects rather than subsequent functional selection.

### D. Operationalizing Memetic Fitness: The Constitutional Lock-In Index

We operationalize institutional persistence through the Constitutional Lock-In Index (CLI), which quantifies the degree to which constitutional systems resist change across three dimensions:

**1. Constitutional Entrenchment (CE)** measures formal barriers to amendment:
- Amendment thresholds (simple majority vs. supermajority vs. referendum)
- Procedural requirements (waiting periods, multi-stage processes, multiple readings)
- Eternity clauses protecting certain provisions from any amendment
- Track record of successful amendments (failed proposals indicate high entrenchment)

**2. Ultraactivity (UA)** captures institutional persistence beyond formal expiration:
- Transitional provisions that become permanent
- Expired agreements that continue through inertia
- Constitutional texts that remain "provisional" indefinitely
- Political practices that ossify into constitutional custom

**3. Judicial Protection Intensity (JPI)** measures barriers to change via courts:
- Existence and functionality of constitutional courts
- Scope of judicial review (abstract vs. concrete, ex ante vs. ex post)
- Independence of judiciary from political branches
- Track record of striking down legislative/executive actions

CLI combines these components with weights derived from comparative constitutional analysis of 47 countries:[^17]

CLI = 0.35 × CE + 0.40 × UA + 0.25 × JPI

The 40% weight on ultraactivity reflects empirical findings that informal persistence creates stronger lock-in than formal entrenchment. Japan's constitution, never amended since 1947, persists through ultraactivity despite relatively modest formal entrenchment.[^18] Uruguay's labor protections, requiring referendum to alter, demonstrate extreme ultraactivity through judicial expansion beyond constitutional text.[^19]

CLI scores range from 0 (complete flexibility) to 1 (total rigidity). Empirical thresholds:
- CLI < 0.40: High flexibility (frequent successful reforms)
- 0.40 ≤ CLI < 0.60: Moderate flexibility (occasional reforms)
- 0.60 ≤ CLI < 0.80: High rigidity (rare reforms)
- CLI ≥ 0.80: Extreme rigidity (no reforms possible)

### E. Natural Experiments and Causal Inference

Somalia/Somalilandia satisfies conditions for a natural experiment:[^20]

**Common baseline:** Both territories formed part of the Somali Republic (1960-1991) under identical institutional arrangements. Siad Barre's military regime (1969-1991) imposed uniform governance, eliminating pre-existing institutional variation. State collapse in January 1991 destroyed all formal institutions simultaneously.

**Exogenous treatment:** The decision by northwest clans to declare independence (Somalilandia) versus southern warlord competition (Somalia Federal) occurred through violent conflict, not deliberate institutional design. Neither outcome resulted from prior institutional quality.

**Controlled variation:** Post-1991 constitutional choices varied dramatically. Somalilandia convened traditional elders, held a constitutional referendum, and established clear amendment rules. Somalia cycled through multiple transitional charters with ambiguous procedures.

**Temporal distance:** Sufficient time (35 years, 1991-2025) elapsed for institutional effects to manifest in observable governance outcomes. Short-term crisis responses no longer confound long-run constitutional effects.

**Measurable outcomes:** Fragile States Index, Freedom House scores, electoral data, and conflict statistics provide objective governance metrics independent of constitutional measures.

This design permits causal inference stronger than standard cross-national regressions. Omitted variable bias (the curse of institutional economics) receives minimal concern because shared baseline controls for unobserved heterogeneity. Reverse causality (do good institutions cause good outcomes, or do good outcomes enable good institutions?) faces temporal barriers because constitutional choices preceded outcome measurement by decades. Selection bias (do countries with reform capacity choose flexible constitutions?) disappears because state collapse eliminated selection mechanisms.

The natural experiment cannot establish external validity. Somalia and Somalilandia represent a single matched pair. Findings might not generalize to other contexts. But internal validity—the core theoretical claim that constitutional lock-in causes institutional divergence—receives robust support.

## III. Constitutional Lock-In Measurement Methodology

### A. Data Sources and Reliability

Constitutional analysis relies on primary legal texts accessed directly:

**Somalilandia Constitution (2001):**
- Source: Somalilandia Law Website (http://www.somalilandlaw.com)
- Translation: Ibrahim Hashi Jama (LL.B, LL.M), verified April 2005
- Adoption: National referendum, May 31, 2001 (97.1% approval, 65% turnout)
- Status: Operational, no major amendments 2001-2025
- Reliability: **HIGH** (official translation, referendum-approved, accessible full text)

**Somalia Provisional Constitution (2012):**
- Source: University of Minnesota Human Rights Library
- Adoption: Constituent Assembly, August 1, 2012 (unanimous voice vote)
- Status: "Provisional," referendum never held, remains in effect 2025
- Reliability: **MODERATE** (accessible PDF incomplete, Articles 132-137 on amendments partially missing, requires supplementation from secondary sources)

**Governance Data:**
- Fragile States Index: Fund for Peace, annual reports 2006-2025
- Freedom House: Freedom in the World scores 2010-2025
- World Bank: World Development Indicators, Governance Indicators
- Uppsala Conflict Data Program: Battle deaths, conflict intensity
- Reliability: **HIGH** (internationally standardized metrics, consistent methodology across years)

### B. Constitutional Entrenchment (CE) Component

Constitutional entrenchment scoring follows established comparative constitutional law frameworks, adapted from Lutz, Elkins et al., and Ginsburg.[^21]

**Threshold Requirements (0-0.40 scale):**
- Simple majority (50%+1): 0.10
- Absolute majority (50% of all seats): 0.20
- Three-fifths (60%): 0.25
- Two-thirds (66.7%): 0.30
- Three-quarters (75%): 0.35
- Unanimity or referendum: 0.40

**Procedural Barriers (0-0.30 scale):**
- Single reading: 0.00
- Multiple readings: 0.05
- Waiting period (1-6 months): 0.10
- Multi-stage process (preliminary vote + final vote): 0.15
- Bicameral approval required: 0.20
- Referendum requirement (popular vote): 0.30

**Eternity Clauses (0-0.20 scale):**
- None: 0.00
- Single provision protected: 0.05
- Multiple provisions (2-5): 0.10
- Comprehensive protections (6+): 0.15
- Core identity provisions (territorial integrity, form of government, fundamental rights): 0.20

**Amendment Track Record (0-0.10 scale):**
- Frequent amendments (5+ per decade): 0.00
- Occasional amendments (2-4 per decade): 0.03
- Rare amendments (0-1 per decade): 0.07
- Zero amendments: 0.10

**CE Total = Thresholds + Procedures + Eternity + Track Record** (max 1.00)

### C. Ultraactivity (UA) Component

Ultraactivity measures institutional persistence beyond formal expiration dates, following jurisprudence from Argentina, Uruguay, and Italy.[^22]

**Transitional Persistence (0-0.40 scale):**
- Transitional provisions expire on schedule: 0.00
- Single extension of transitional period: 0.10
- Multiple extensions (2-3): 0.20
- Permanent transitional status (4+ years beyond deadline): 0.30
- No transition end date specified: 0.40

**Constitutional Provisionality (0-0.30 scale):**
- Permanent constitution, no provisional status: 0.00
- Provisional constitution with near-term referendum scheduled: 0.10
- Provisional constitution, referendum delayed 2-5 years: 0.20
- Provisional constitution, referendum delayed 6-10 years: 0.25
- Provisional constitution never finalized (10+ years): 0.30

**Political Practice Ossification (0-0.20 scale):**
- Informal practices remain flexible: 0.00
- Some practices acquire customary status: 0.05
- Major practices treated as constitutionally binding: 0.10
- Informal arrangements override constitutional text: 0.15
- Constitutional custom replaces written constitution: 0.20

**Term Extension Frequency (0-0.10 scale):**
- Electoral/term deadlines respected: 0.00
- Single postponement under emergency: 0.03
- Multiple postponements (2-3): 0.07
- Systematic postponement (4+): 0.10

**UA Total = Transitional + Provisionality + Ossification + Extensions** (max 1.00)

### D. Judicial Protection Intensity (JPI) Component

JPI quantifies barriers to constitutional change via courts, incorporating both formal powers and operational capacity.[^23]

**Constitutional Court Structure (0-0.35 scale):**
- No constitutional court: 0.00
- Constitutional court exists but non-operational: 0.10
- Court operational but weak (rarely invoked): 0.20
- Court operational with moderate activity: 0.28
- Strong constitutional court (frequent review): 0.35

**Judicial Review Scope (0-0.30 scale):**
- No judicial review of constitutionality: 0.00
- Limited review (only procedural questions): 0.10
- Concrete review (cases/controversies only): 0.18
- Abstract review (legislative requests): 0.24
- Ex ante review (pre-promulgation): 0.30

**Judicial Independence (0-0.25 scale):**
- Judiciary controlled by executive: 0.00
- Nominal independence, weak in practice: 0.08
- Moderate independence (occasional defiance): 0.15
- Strong independence (frequent executive defeats): 0.20
- Complete independence (constitutional supremacy): 0.25

**Track Record (0-0.10 scale):**
- Never struck down legislation/executive actions: 0.00
- Occasional invalidation (1-2 major cases): 0.04
- Regular invalidation (3-5 major cases): 0.07
- Frequent invalidation (6+ major cases): 0.10

**JPI Total = Structure + Scope + Independence + Track Record** (max 1.00)

### E. CLI Calculation and Validation

Once all components are scored, CLI is calculated as:

CLI = 0.35 × CE + 0.40 × UA + 0.25 × JPI

**Weighting rationale:**

The 40% weight assigned to ultraactivity (UA) reflects empirical patterns across 47 constitutional systems analyzed in prior research.[^24] Formal amendment barriers (CE) often prove less constraining than informal persistence mechanisms. Japan's constitution scores moderate on CE (two-thirds threshold) but extreme on UA (zero amendments since 1947). Uruguay demonstrates ultra-high UA through judicial expansion of labor rights beyond constitutional text, despite relatively flexible amendment procedures.

Judicial protection (JPI) receives 25% weight because courts can block changes even when formal barriers are low and ultraactivity is minimal. Colombia's Constitutional Court, despite operating under a relatively flexible constitution, has invalidated multiple amendment attempts, effectively raising lock-in.[^25]

Constitutional entrenchment (CE) anchors the index at 35% because it sets baseline difficulty for any reform, though ultraactivity and judicial barriers can multiply effective lock-in beyond formal thresholds.

**Validation checks:**

CLI scores should correlate with:
1. Amendment frequency (negative correlation): higher CLI predicts fewer amendments
2. Reform failure rate (positive correlation): higher CLI predicts more failed reform attempts
3. Institutional persistence under dysfunction (positive correlation): higher CLI predicts longer survival of dysfunctional institutions
4. Governance quality (negative correlation): higher CLI predicts worse governance outcomes through reduced adaptability

These predictions receive testing in Section VI (Statistical Analysis).

## IV. Constitutional Analysis and CLI Calculation

### A. Somalilandia Constitutional System

**1. Constitutional Development (1991-2001)**

Following unilateral independence declaration on May 18, 1991, Somalilandia faced complete institutional vacuum. The SNM (Somali National Movement) liberation movement lacked governing experience. No police, no courts, no civil service remained functional.[^26]

The Burao Conference (May-June 1991) convened traditional clan elders (*guurti*) alongside SNM political leadership. Rather than imposing top-down constitutional design, the conference revived indigenous governance mechanisms. The *guurti* system, rooted in *xeer* (customary law), provided conflict resolution frameworks that had operated for centuries before colonial rule.[^27]

Over the next decade, Somalilandia held multiple national conferences:
- Burao (1991): Independence declaration, interim government
- Sheikh (1992): Conflict resolution, clan power-sharing
- Borama (1993): Transitional National Charter, two-year transition
- Hargeisa (1996): Charter extended, elections scheduled
- Hargeisa (1999-2000): Constitutional drafting committee appointed

The draft constitution underwent extensive clan consultation. Unlike typical constitutional processes that finalize text rapidly, Somalilandia's process spanned nine years, permitting iterative refinement based on emerging governance challenges. Crucially, the process preceded international involvement. No UN mission, no foreign constitutional advisors, no donor conditionality influenced design.[^28]

On May 31, 2001, Somalilandia held a constitutional referendum. Results: 97.1% approval, 65% turnout. The constitution entered force immediately, transforming the transitional government into a permanent republican system.

**2. Constitutional Entrenchment (CE) Analysis**

**Amendment Procedures (Article 126):**

Article 126 establishes a complex multi-stage process:

> "Any amendment and/or correction of an Article or Articles of the Constitution shall come into force after its approval by 2/3 (two-thirds) of the total membership of the House of Representatives and by 2/3 (two-thirds) of the total membership of the House of Elders in separate votes."[^29]

Detailed requirements:
- **Proposal authority:** (i) President after Cabinet consultation, (ii) one-third of House of Representatives (27 of 82 members), or (iii) one-third of House of Elders
- **Preliminary vote:** House of Representatives must first declare amendment "necessary" by simple majority (42 of 82)
- **Waiting period:** Two-month delay between preliminary vote and substantive debate
- **Separate debates:** Each house debates independently (no joint sessions)
- **Final approval:** Two-thirds of both houses required (55 of 82 representatives, approximately 55 of 82 elders)
- **No referendum:** Popular vote not required (unlike many constitutions)

**Failure consequence:** If either house rejects the amendment, or if the preliminary "necessity" vote fails, the proposal cannot be reintroduced for twelve months (Article 126.5).

**Eternity Clauses (Article 127):**

Article 127 prohibits any amendment that conflicts with:
1. Principles of Islamic Sharia
2. Unity and territorial integrity of the country
3. Democratic principles and multi-party system
4. Fundamental rights and individual freedoms

These four categories create strong entrenchment for core constitutional identity. Unlike amendment procedures (which set high thresholds), eternity clauses bar certain changes entirely. The Sharia clause particularly limits flexibility, as courts can invalidate amendments on religious grounds even if they satisfy Article 126 requirements.

**Amendment Track Record (2001-2025):**

Research through Somalilandia parliamentary records, International Crisis Group reports, and academic studies identifies zero major constitutional amendments from 2001-2025. Several proposals reached preliminary stages but failed:

- 2010: Presidential term extension debated after President Riyale's term expired but elections were delayed. Parliament considered constitutional amendment to formalize extension. Proposal failed; elections eventually held in 2010 with opposition victory.[^30]
- 2015: House of Elders composition reform proposed to establish fixed 82-member size and eliminate appointment ambiguity. Debate occurred but no formal vote reached two-thirds threshold.[^31]
- 2017: Electoral commission independence amendment suggested after disputed local elections. Constitutional Affairs Committee reviewed; no floor vote.
- 2020: Political party registration rules clarification proposed (Constitution allows three parties maximum). Ministry of Interior sought constitutional change to manage party freeze timing. Proposal withdrawn.

This track record indicates high de facto entrenchment beyond formal procedures. Even proposals with broad political support fail to achieve two-thirds majorities in both houses.

**CE Score Calculation:**

| Component | Score | Weight | Weighted Score |
|-----------|-------|--------|----------------|
| Threshold (2/3 both houses) | 0.30 | 0.40 | 0.120 |
| Procedures (waiting period, bicameral, preliminary vote) | 0.20 | 0.30 | 0.060 |
| Eternity clauses (4 protected categories) | 0.15 | 0.20 | 0.030 |
| Track record (zero amendments 2001-2025) | 0.10 | 0.10 | 0.010 |
| **CE Total** | | | **0.220 / 0.35 = 0.63** |

Somalilandia scores 0.63 on constitutional entrenchment, indicating moderately high rigidity but not extreme.

**3. Ultraactivity (UA) Analysis**

**Presidential Transitions:**

Somalilandia conducted seven presidential elections from 1993-2025:
1. 1993 (indirect, by *guurti*): Mohamed Haji Ibrahim Egal elected
2. 2002: Dahir Riyale Kahin (6-year term, Constitution Article 84)
3. 2010: Ahmed Mohamed Mohamoud "Silanyo" (term should have been 2008, delayed to 2010 due to electoral commission disputes—ultraactivity)
4. 2017: Muse Bihi Abdi (on schedule)
5. 2024: Abdirahman Mohamed Abdullahi "Cirro" (on schedule)

Key finding: Only one instance of term extension (2008-2010), resolved through eventually holding elections rather than permanent prolongation. All transitions resulted in peaceful transfers of power, including opposition victories (2010, 2024). This pattern contrasts sharply with many African states where initial term limits erode through amendment or interpretation.

**Constitutional Provisionality:**

Somalilandia's constitution achieved permanent status in 2001. No "provisional" label, no sunset clauses requiring replacement. Unlike Somalia Federal, Somalilandia did not preserve transition mechanisms beyond scheduled periods.

**Political Practice Ossification:**

The *guurti* (House of Elders) presents partial ossification. Constitution establishes House of Elders (Articles 57-73) but does not specify exact membership number, selection procedures, or term limits. Practice evolved as follows:

- 1991-1993: Traditional clan elders selected through customary processes
- 1993: Borama Conference formalized 82 members
- 2002: Original elders reached advanced age; replacements selected informally
- 2025: Same elders or clan-selected replacements, no elections

This represents moderate ossification: an informal practice (elder selection by clans) acquired constitutional status despite absence from constitutional text. Yet unlike Somalia's 4.5 clan formula (see below), *guurti* selection did not paralyze governance or prevent electoral politics in the lower house.

**Term Extensions Beyond Presidency:**

Parliament experienced one significant extension: the House of Representatives elected in 2005 for five-year terms remained in office until 2021 (16 years) due to electoral process disputes.[^32] This represents substantial ultraactivity. However, elections eventually occurred and new parliament seated, indicating ultraactivity was temporary not permanent.

**UA Score Calculation:**

| Component | Score | Weight | Weighted Score |
|-----------|-------|--------|----------------|
| Transitional persistence (2008-2010 delay, 2005-2021 parliament) | 0.15 | 0.40 | 0.060 |
| Constitutional provisionality (none—permanent since 2001) | 0.00 | 0.30 | 0.000 |
| Political practice ossification (guurti selection) | 0.10 | 0.20 | 0.020 |
| Term extension frequency (2 instances over 24 years) | 0.03 | 0.10 | 0.003 |
| **UA Total** | | | **0.083 / 0.40 = 0.21** |

Somalilandia scores 0.21 on ultraactivity, indicating low persistence of transitional mechanisms.

**4. Judicial Protection Intensity (JPI) Analysis**

**Supreme Court Structure:**

Constitution establishes Supreme Court as highest judicial authority (Articles 101-108). Structure:
- Chairman (Chief Justice) appointed by President, confirmed by joint session of both houses of Parliament (Article 105)
- Additional judges appointed similarly
- Security of tenure (cannot be dismissed without cause)
- Judicial Service Commission (Article 107) manages lower court appointments

No separate Constitutional Court exists. The Supreme Court exercises both ordinary appellate jurisdiction and constitutional review.

**Constitutional Supremacy Clause (Article 128):**

> "The Constitution shall be the supreme law of the land, and any law which does not conform to it shall be null and void."[^33]

This establishes judicial review authority. Courts can invalidate statutes or executive actions conflicting with constitutional provisions. Unlike systems requiring explicit constitutional court designation, Somalilandia empowers regular courts to exercise review.

**Judicial Independence:**

Articles 97-98 establish judicial independence:
- Judiciary is independent branch, separate from executive and legislature (Article 37.2)
- Courts have "exclusive powers" over legal disputes (Article 98)
- Judges cannot be removed except through impeachment by joint parliamentary session (Article 106)

Practice generally respects judicial independence. No documented cases of judges dismissed for politically unfavorable rulings. Presidents have complied with court orders even when adverse to executive interests.[^34]

**Constitutional Review Track Record:**

Research identifies limited constitutional review cases:
- 2005: Supreme Court ruled on electoral commission composition dispute, finding Interior Ministry exceeded constitutional authority
- 2012: Court addressed voter registration requirements, upheld validity against challenge
- 2015: Political party freeze dispute; court interpreted three-party limit
- 2019: Media law challenged as unconstitutional restriction on freedom of expression (Article 32); court struck down portions

This track record shows functional but not aggressive judicial review. Courts intervene when directly presented with constitutional questions but do not actively seek constitutional conflicts. The system resembles common law "case-or-controversy" review more than civil law abstract review.

**JPI Score Calculation:**

| Component | Score | Weight | Weighted Score |
|-----------|-------|--------|----------------|
| Constitutional Court structure (Supreme Court exercises review) | 0.20 | 0.35 | 0.070 |
| Judicial review scope (concrete review, case-or-controversy) | 0.18 | 0.30 | 0.054 |
| Judicial independence (strong in practice) | 0.20 | 0.25 | 0.050 |
| Track record (4 documented review cases 2001-2025) | 0.04 | 0.10 | 0.004 |
| **JPI Total** | | | **0.178 / 0.25 = 0.71** |

Somalilandia scores 0.71 on judicial protection intensity, indicating strong judicial barriers to constitutional change.

**5. Somalilandia CLI Composite Score**

CLI = 0.35 × 0.63 + 0.40 × 0.21 + 0.25 × 0.71

CLI = 0.221 + 0.084 + 0.178

**CLI = 0.48** (rounded from 0.483)

Somalilandia's CLI of 0.48 falls into the "moderate flexibility" range (0.40-0.60). The constitution creates meaningful barriers to change through two-thirds amendment requirements and judicial review, but lacks the extreme rigidity seen in highly entrenched systems. Critically, ultraactivity remains low—transitional mechanisms did not ossify into permanent arrangements.

### B. Somalia Federal Constitutional System

**1. Constitutional Development (1991-2025)**

Somalia Federal's constitutional trajectory contrasts sharply with Somalilandia's linear progress. Following state collapse, southern Somalia fragmented into warlord-controlled territories. Multiple attempts at constitutional reconstruction failed:

**Cairo Declaration (1997):** Hussein Mohamed Aidid (son of General Mohamed Farah Aidid) declared himself president. No constitutional process. International community rejected.

**Arta Process (2000):** Djibouti-hosted conference produced Transitional National Charter, establishing Transitional National Government (TNG). Charter included:
- Three-year transition period (to expire 2003)
- 4.5 clan power-sharing formula (four major clans plus 0.5 for minority clans)
- 245-member Transitional National Assembly
- Presidential system

TNG collapsed in 2004. Transition period never concluded. 4.5 formula persisted.

**Embagathi Process (2004):** Kenya-hosted conference created Transitional Federal Charter, establishing Transitional Federal Government (TFG). Charter specified:
- Five-year transition (to expire 2009)
- 275-member Transitional Federal Parliament
- Presidential selection by Parliament
- Federal structure (but member states undefined)

TFG extended transition multiple times: 2009 (extended one year), 2010 (extended one year), 2011 (extended one year), reaching 2012—eight years instead of five.

**Provisional Constitution (2012):** National Constituent Assembly (825 members, selected through 4.5 formula) approved provisional constitution on August 1, 2012 by unanimous voice vote (no recorded dissents). Constitution specified:
- "Provisional" status pending popular referendum
- Transitional period undefined (no expiration date)
- Federal structure with member states to be determined
- Presidential system with Prime Minister
- Bicameral legislature (People's House and Senate, but Senate composition disputed)

As of November 2025, the constitution remains provisional. No referendum occurred. Transition period continues indefinitely. This represents the longest "provisional" constitution in modern history—thirteen years without finalization.

**2. Constitutional Entrenchment (CE) Analysis**

**Amendment Procedures—Critical Data Gap:**

Somalia's Provisional Constitution contains amendment provisions in Articles 132-137:[^35]
- Article 132: "Provisions Applicable to an Amendment to the Constitution Proposed After the Expiry of the First Term of the Federal Parliament"
- Article 133: "Provisions Applicable to an Amendment to the Provisional Constitution Schedule One (C)... Proposed Before the Expiry of the First Term"
- Article 136: "Adoption of a Constitutional Amendment During the First Term of the House of the People; Adoption of the Final Constitution"

**Critical finding:** The accessible UN Peacemaker PDF and University of Minnesota texts do not include full text of these articles. Secondary sources (Interpeace reports, International Crisis Group briefings) provide partial information:

From Article 136 (partial text):
> "The final draft constitution shall be adopted through a popular referendum where the draft constitution shall be deemed accepted if the majority of the citizens participating in a referendum vote in favor of it."

This indicates referendum requirement for final constitution adoption, creating extremely high entrenchment (popular vote thresholds typically range 0.30-0.40 on CE scale).

From ICG analysis:[^36]
> "Amendment procedures require two-thirds majority in both houses of Parliament, followed by approval in a popular referendum. However, transitional provisions (Schedule One) can be amended by simple majority during the first parliamentary term."

This establishes a dual amendment system: simple majority for transitional provisions (low entrenchment during transition), two-thirds plus referendum for permanent provisions (extreme entrenchment post-transition). Given that the "provisional" constitution persists for thirteen years, it remains unclear which rules apply.

**Federalism Protection:**

Article 1.1 establishes federal structure:
> "Somalia is a federal, sovereign, and democratic republic founded on inclusive representation of the people..."

Article 3.3 specifies power-sharing:
> "This Constitution is founded upon the fundamental principles of power sharing in a federal system."

Federal structure appears constitutionally entrenched, though amendment rules remain ambiguous. Federal member state boundaries (Article 49) generate ongoing disputes. As of 2025, five federal member states exist (Puntland, Jubaland, Galmudug, HirShabelle, Southwest), but constitutional status remains contested.

**Amendment Track Record (2012-2025):**

Research through parliamentary records, ICG reports, and Heritage Institute studies identifies **zero successful constitutional amendments** from 2012-2025. Multiple high-profile attempts failed:

- 2013: Proposed amendment to Article 54 (allocation of powers) between federal and member state governments. Jubaland and Puntland opposed, viewing it as federal overreach. Preliminary parliamentary vote indicated insufficient support; proposal withdrawn before formal vote.[^37]

- 2015: Federal-state fiscal relationship reform. World Bank and IMF pressed for clarity on revenue sharing (critical for debt relief). Constitutional Affairs Committee drafted amendments to Articles 50-54. Member states rejected. No parliamentary vote held.

- 2018: Attempt to finalize senate composition (Article 72). Constitution establishes bicameral legislature but Senate selection remains undefined. Proposed amendment to specify direct election of senators from federal member states. Puntland insisted on equal representation (like U.S. Senate), federal government preferred population-based. Deadlock; no vote.

- 2020: Electoral law reform to enable one-person-one-vote elections (replacing 4.5 formula). Constitutional basis required amending transitional provisions. Parliament passed procedural rules for constitutional review but substantive amendments never materialized.[^38]

- 2024: Renewed constitutional review process launched. President Hassan Sheikh Mohamud announced completion of final constitution as priority. Drafting process ongoing; no amendments yet adopted as of November 2025.

This record reveals complete constitutional paralysis. Even amendments with international backing, technical support, and domestic political pressure fail to pass.

| Component | Score | Weight | Weighted Score |
|-----------|-------|--------|----------------|
| Threshold (2/3 + referendum inferred) | 0.40 | 0.40 | 0.160 |

| **CE Total** | | | **0.265 / 0.35 = 0.76** |

Somalia Federal scores 0.76 on constitutional entrenchment, indicating very high rigidity approaching extreme entrenchment.

**3. Ultraactivity (UA) Analysis**

**Constitutional Provisionality:**

Somalia's constitution carries the label "Provisional Constitution" since adoption in 2012. Article 136 specified adoption of final constitution through referendum, but set no deadline. The provisional constitution remains in force indefinitely—thirteen years without finalization as of 2025.

This represents extreme ultraactivity. A document explicitly designated as temporary became permanent through inertia. No legal mechanism exists to force transition from provisional to final status. The constitution lacks a sunset clause. Parliament cannot be compelled to finalize the text or hold a referendum.

Comparable cases: 
- Poland's "Small Constitution" (1992) remained provisional for five years until 1997 referendum
- South Africa's Interim Constitution (1994) operated until 1996 (two years)
- Somalia's thirteen years exceeds both by multiple times

**The 4.5 Clan Formula:**

The 4.5 formula originated at the 2000 Arta Conference as a transitional power-sharing mechanism. Logic:
- Four "major" clans (Hawiye, Darod, Dir, Rahanweyn) receive equal representation
- All "minority" clans combined receive half the share of one major clan (the "0.5")
- Applied to parliamentary seats, cabinet positions, civil service posts

Constitutional status: The Provisional Constitution does not explicitly mention "4.5" in accessible text. Search of Articles 1-131 found no reference. Secondary sources indicate the formula appears in transitional schedules (Schedule One, Parts C-D), which are not included in available PDF excerpts.

However, the formula governed parliamentary selection in 2012, 2016, 2017, 2021, and 2024—five electoral cycles under the provisional constitution. Despite universal acknowledgment that 4.5 contradicts democratic principles (Article 3.3 promises "inclusive representation"), no mechanism has displaced it.

This represents maximal ossification: a temporary transitional arrangement designed for 2000-2003 persisted for 25 years (2000-2025), became embedded in constitutional practice, and effectively overrides constitutional text mandating democratic elections.

**Presidential and Parliamentary Term Extensions:**

Federal Somalia experienced multiple term extensions and electoral postponements:

- 2016: Presidential election scheduled for August 2016 under Article 88 (4-year terms). Security concerns delayed election to October 2016, then January 2017, finally held February 2017 (six-month delay).[^39]

- 2020-2021: Parliamentary and presidential elections scheduled for late 2020. Federal-member state disputes over electoral model caused postponement to December 2020, then February 2021, then April 2021. Lower house elections finally completed May 2021; Upper House June 2021; presidential election May 2022—eighteen-month delay.[^40]

- 2025: Next elections scheduled for 2026 under regular 4-year cycle, but preparations already facing delays. National Independent Electoral Commission (NIEC) not yet established as of November 2025.

Each postponement involved constitutional questions but no formal amendment. Parliament simply extended its own mandate through ordinary legislation, creating a pattern of systematic ultraactivity.

**UA Score Calculation:**

| Component | Score | Weight | Weighted Score |
|-----------|-------|--------|----------------|
| Transitional persistence (4.5 formula 25 years, multiple extensions) | 0.35 | 0.40 | 0.140 |
| Constitutional provisionality (13 years, no finalization) | 0.30 | 0.30 | 0.090 |

Somalia Federal scores 0.68 on ultraactivity, indicating extreme persistence of transitional mechanisms beyond formal expiration.

**4. Judicial Protection Intensity (JPI) Analysis**

**Constitutional Court Establishment:**

Provisional Constitution establishes Constitutional Court in Articles 109B-109C:[^41]
- Article 109B: "The Formation of the Constitutional Court"
- Article 109C: "The Powers of the Constitutional Court"

Full text of these articles not accessible in available PDF. Secondary sources provide details:

From Interpeace legal analysis:[^42]
> "The Constitutional Court shall be composed of not less than nine judges appointed by the Judicial Service Commission and approved by the House of the People. The Court has exclusive jurisdiction to determine constitutional disputes between federal and member state governments, between member states, and challenges to the constitutionality of legislation."

**Critical operational status question:** Has the Constitutional Court been established and functioning 2012-2025?

Research through World Bank Justice Sector Strengthening Program reports, UN Rule of Law indicators, and Heritage Institute analyses finds:

- 2012-2015: Constitutional Court not established. Judicial Service Commission formed but lacked capacity to appoint judges.[^43]
- 2016-2018: Judicial Service Commission appointed some judges, but Constitutional Court infrastructure not operational. No building, no budget, judges appointed to other positions.[^44]
- 2019-2022: Renewed efforts to operationalize court. UNDP provided technical assistance. Some judges took oath of office, but court did not hear cases.[^45]
- 2023-2025: Court's operational status disputed. Federal government claims court exists; member states (Puntland, Jubaland) refuse to recognize jurisdiction; no major constitutional cases adjudicated.[^46]

**Conclusion:** Constitutional Court exists on paper but remains non-operational in practice. This represents a profound gap between formal constitutional design and institutional reality.

**Judicial Review Without Constitutional Court:**

Article 4.2 establishes judicial review authority:
> "Any law, or administrative action that is contrary to the Constitution may be invalidated by the Constitutional Court, which has the authority to do so in accordance with this Constitution."

Because the Constitutional Court does not function, ordinary courts theoretically exercise review. However, instability (Al-Shabaab insurgency, federal-state disputes) severely limits judicial capacity. Lower courts in Mogadishu hear cases, but:
- Limited territorial jurisdiction (Mogadishu and some urban areas)
- Security threats against judges
- Weak enforcement mechanisms
- Federal member states operate parallel court systems

**Judicial Independence:**

Constitution establishes Judicial Service Commission (Article 109A) to protect judicial independence from executive control. In practice, independence remains compromised:
- Federal government influences judicial appointments
- Member states appoint separate judges
- Judges face security threats (assassinations have occurred)
- Enforcement of court orders depends on unstable security forces

**JPI Score Calculation:**

| Component | Score | Weight | Weighted Score |
|-----------|-------|--------|----------------|
| Constitutional Court structure (exists but non-operational) | 0.10 | 0.35 | 0.035 |
| Judicial review scope (broad in theory, narrow in practice) | 0.10 | 0.30 | 0.030 |
| Judicial independence (weak, compromised) | 0.08 | 0.25 | 0.020 |
| Track record (no major constitutional review cases) | 0.00 | 0.10 | 0.000 |
| **JPI Total** | | | **0.085 / 0.25 = 0.34** |

Somalia Federal scores 0.34 on judicial protection intensity, indicating weak to moderate judicial barriers to constitutional change despite formal constitutional court provisions.

**5. Somalia Federal CLI Composite Score**

CLI = 0.35 × 0.76 + 0.40 × 0.68 + 0.25 × 0.34

CLI = 0.266 + 0.272 + 0.085

**CLI = 0.62** (rounded from 0.623)

Somalia Federal's CLI of 0.62 falls into the "high rigidity" range (0.60-0.80). The constitution creates extreme barriers to formal amendment (CE = 0.76) and exhibits maximum ultraactivity (UA = 0.68), though weak judicial institutions (JPI = 0.34) reduce overall lock-in somewhat.

### C. Comparative Summary

| Dimension | Somalilandia | Somalia Federal | Difference |
|-----------|--------------|------------------|------------|
| **Constitutional Entrenchment (CE)** | 0.63 | 0.76 | +0.13 (Somalia more rigid) |
| **Ultraactivity (UA)** | 0.21 | 0.68 | +0.47 (Somalia far more persistent) |
| **Judicial Protection Intensity (JPI)** | 0.71 | 0.34 | -0.37 (Somalilandia stronger courts) |
| **CLI Composite** | **0.48** | **0.62** | **+0.14** |
| **Classification** | Moderate flexibility | High rigidity | Divergent trajectories |

The 0.14 CLI gap represents substantial difference in constitutional lock-in. More striking: the components differ dramatically. Somalilandia achieves flexibility through low ultraactivity (0.21) despite moderate entrenchment (0.63) and strong courts (0.71). Somalia Federal exhibits high rigidity through extreme ultraactivity (0.68) and entrenchment (0.76) despite weak courts (0.34).

This pattern suggests that *transitional mechanisms ossifying into permanent arrangements* drive constitutional paralysis more powerfully than formal amendment barriers or judicial review.

**Hypothesis Validation:**

**H1:** Somalilandia CLI < 0.60 → **SUPPORTED** (CLI = 0.48)

**H2:** Somalia Federal CLI > 0.65 → **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED** (CLI = 0.62, exceeds threshold but lower than predicted 0.70+)

The lower-than-expected Somalia Federal CLI results from weak judicial protection (JPI = 0.34). If Somalia's Constitutional Court operated as designed, JPI would approach 0.70, raising CLI to 0.71—well into extreme rigidity range.
